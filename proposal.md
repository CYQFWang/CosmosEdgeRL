## Research Question
This project investigates post-training world-action models (WAMs) with reinforcement learning (RL). Specifically, we ask whether the world-prediction capability of WAM can provide useful intermediate reward signals, and whether improving the alignment between predicted states and actual states can improve the resulting action policy.
## Motivation
Most vision-language-action policies are trained primarily through imitation learning, which requires large amounts of demonstration data. Collecting such data is expensive and difficult to scale. In contrast, RL allows a policy to generate its own rollouts and improve through interaction with a simulation environment. Modern GPUs can execute many environments in parallel, making large-scale RL training substantially more practical.
A pretrained WAM provides a promising starting point. Rather than learning from scratch, we can start from a model that already jointly understands observations, future states, and robot actions. Existing work on world-model-based policies also suggests substantial cross-modality and cross-embodiment generalization, motivating the question of whether these capabilities can be preserved or even strengthened during RL.
## Proposed Approach
Our main direction is to exploit the next-state prediction component of a WAM as an auxiliary source of reward or supervision. Given an observation and generated action, the WAM predicts the subsequent visual state. During simulation, the corresponding true next state is directly observable. We will construct sub-task or auxiliary rewards based on the alignment between the predicted and actual next states and study whether higher world-prediction consistency correlates with and contributes to better action generation.
A systematic ablation study will compare feasible reward signals and training strategies. Key comparisons will include task-only rewards versus task rewards augmented with prediction-alignment rewards, different formulations and weights of the auxiliary reward, and alternative choices of trainable model components during post-training.
## Data, Models, and Evaluation
Experiments will primarily use ManiSkill3 and RoboLab, together with other suitable simulation tasks when necessary. ManiSkill3 provides roughly 20 tabletop two-finger-gripper tasks and an eight-task small-set benchmark. RoboLab provides a larger benchmark library of 120 tasks, with three instruction levels—default, vague, and specific—allowing controlled train/test splits and evaluation of instruction-level generalization.
Our primary model will be Cosmos3-Edge-Policy-DROID, a 4B WAM  that jointly generates future video states and robot actions. We will also consider a smaller model, such as V-JEPA2-AC, as a backup option requiring lower computational cost or faster iteration.
Evaluation will focus on two dimensions: final test-set task performance and RL training behavior, including convergence speed, stability, sample efficiency, and generalization to held-out tasks or instruction variants.
## Feasibility
Both ManiSkill3 and RoboLab support simulation and parallel environment execution, which makes them suitable for RL rollout collection. Our approach also avoids training a world-action model from scratch: most computation is devoted to post-training an already capable pretrained policy. Experiments will be conducted using two NVIDIA H800 GPUs.
Preliminary tests on a subset suggest that a full training run can be completed in approximately 13 hours, making repeated experiments and ablations feasible within the project period. The number of training tasks, parallel environments, rollout length, and trainable model parameters can be reduced if computational cost becomes limiting.
